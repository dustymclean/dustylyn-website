Timely and Untimely Applicants

The DEA’s medical marijuana registration rules and the legal questions they raise.

November 26, 2026By Dusty Lyn McLeanGovernment sources only
← All posts

DEA guidance issued October 30, 2026 sorts state medical marijuana operators into two groups by one date: June 26, 2026. Businesses that filed a complete federal application by then are “Timely Applicants” and may keep operating; according to the guidance, timely applicants and registrants may not do business with anyone else (DEA Timely Applicant Information, Oct. 30, 2026).

The DEA set November 29, 2026 as the date by which timely applicants must have a compliant supply chain in place (DEA). On November 25, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit administratively stayed that guidance as it applies to timely applicants doing business with operators who are neither timely nor registered (D.C. Cir. Nos. 26-1324, 26-1325, 26-1329). An administrative stay is temporary and is not a ruling on who is right.

Below is what happened, who falls on which side of the line, and the legal questions the record raises. Every fact is tied to a federal, state or court record listed at the end.

How we got here

Over seven months, the federal government moved state medical marijuana to Schedule III, opened a 60-day application window, and later tied interim operating permission to that window.

Timely, untimely, and never applied

The only thing separating a “timely” operator from an “untimely” one is the date a complete application and fee reached the DEA. Both hold the same state license.

StatusWhoWhat the DEA says they may do
DEA registrantHolds a Schedule III medical marijuana registration (first issued Oct. 30, 2026).Operate; deal with registrants and timely applicants.
Timely applicantFiled a complete application, with fee, Apr. 28–June 26, 2026; application still pending (not withdrawn, not denied by final order); holds a state medical license.Keep dispensing, manufacturing or distributing under the state license while pending; deal only with registrants and other timely applicants.
Untimely applicantFiled after June 26, 2026.Not covered by the interim permission. Treated the same as a non-applicant until registered.
Non-applicantNever filed.Registrants and timely applicants may not deal with them in Schedule III medical marijuana.

The final rule’s own text gives the interim permission to “any applicant that submits an application within 60 days” and says such applicants may operate “in conformity with a state-issued license during the pendency of the application” (21 CFR 1301.13(k)(7), 91 FR 22714). The rule does not say what happens to licensees who file later. The DEA guidance does not use the word “untimely”; it groups late filers with those who never applied (DEA).

What the October 30 guidance requires

The four-page guidance limits who timely applicants and registrants may trade with and lists denial or revocation as possible consequences. The DEA states the document does “not have the force and effect of law” (DEA).

  • Closed trading circle. Timely applicants and registrants may deal only with each other. Dealing with anyone else “could be potential grounds for denial of a Timely Applicant’s application or revocation of a certificate of registration.”
  • Deadline. Timely applicants are “expected to complete these activities and establish compliant supply chains no later than November 29, 2026.”
  • Due diligence on every partner. Get each partner’s DEA submission confirmation and verify its document control number (DCN) on the DEA Diversion website, or get a copy of its DEA certificate; confirm its state license is active; keep records of all of it.
  • Old inventory. Product bought before the guidance, even from non-qualifying sources, may still be sold.
  • Testing labs. Sending samples to a non-DEA-registered lab is tolerated only if the lab is in-state, records are kept, and using a DEA-registered lab would violate state law. Analytical labs get no interim permission at all.

Source for every point above: DEA Medical Marijuana Timely Applicant Information.

Legal questions the record raises

The public record raises seven legal questions. Each is listed with the law it comes from and what the record shows. No court has ruled on the merits of any of them.

1. Notice and comment before a binding rule

Where it lives: The Administrative Procedure Act requires agencies to publish proposed rules and take public comment before they bind anyone (5 U.S.C. 553).

What the record shows: The April order was issued without notice and comment; the DEA said the APA’s notice-and-comment requirements did not apply because it acted by order (91 FR 22714). The October 30 guidance then set a deadline and listed denial and revocation as possible consequences, while stating it is not binding (DEA).

The question: Whether a document that sets a deadline and names consequences functions as a rule that required public comment.

2. An agency following its own rule

Where it lives: Courts must set aside agency action that is arbitrary, not in accordance with law, or beyond statutory authority (5 U.S.C. 706(2)).

What the record shows: The rule lets timely applicants operate “in conformity with a state-issued license during the pendency of the application” (21 CFR 1301.13(k)(7)). It contains no trading-partner condition and no November 29 deadline. The guidance added both.

The question: Whether guidance can add conditions that the published rule does not contain.

3. Fair notice (due process)

Where it lives: No person shall be “deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law” (U.S. Const. amend. V).

What the record shows: The window closed June 26, 2026. The consequence of missing it, that registrants and timely applicants may not trade with late filers, was published October 30, 2026 (DEA). The rule itself never said what happens to licensees who file later (91 FR 22714). In Mississippi, the Health Department told cultivation, processing and transport licensees on August 4 that the DEA still had no portal for their license types (MSDH letter, Aug. 4, 2026).

The question: Whether operators had fair notice of the consequences of missing the window, given that those consequences were published about four months after it closed.

4. The grounds Congress wrote

Where it lives: Congress set the standards for granting a registration (21 U.S.C. 823) and the grounds for revoking one (21 U.S.C. 824).

What the record shows: The guidance names a new reason to deny or revoke: buying from or selling to a state-licensed operator who missed the window (DEA).

The question: Whether dealing with a state licensee who filed late is a valid statutory ground for denial or revocation when it appears only in guidance.

5. Equal treatment of identical licensees

Where it lives: The Fifth Amendment’s due process clause carries an equal-protection component that binds federal agencies (U.S. Const. amend. V).

What the record shows: Two businesses holding the same state license, under the same state program, are treated differently under the guidance based only on their filing date (DEA).

The question: Whether a filing date is a rational basis for treating identical state licensees differently. Courts give agencies broad deference on this kind of question.

6. Small-business analysis

Where it lives: The Regulatory Flexibility Act requires agencies to weigh a rule’s impact on small entities (5 U.S.C. 601–612).

What the record shows: The DEA said the RFA “does not apply to this action” because the order skipped notice and comment. It called the order “not a significant regulatory action” while acknowledging it “may have unique economic impacts” (91 FR 22714). The October guidance contains no small-business analysis.

The question: Whether the economic effect on small state licensees should have been analyzed.

7. A decision in a reasonable time

Where it lives: Agencies must conclude matters “within a reasonable time” (5 U.S.C. 555(b)), and courts can compel action “unreasonably delayed” (5 U.S.C. 706(1)).

What the record shows: The rule says the Administrator “shall make every effort to process all applications submitted within 60 days” within six months (21 CFR 1301.13(k)(7)). As of October 30, registrations had gone to dispensaries in seven states; manufacturer and distributor applications were still under review (DOJ press release 26-1276).

The question: Whether the pace of manufacturer and distributor review fits the rule’s six-month processing goal, given that registered dispensaries depend on those suppliers.

The DEA’s position

The DEA’s position is that the order rests on treaty obligations under the Single Convention, which let it act without the usual rulemaking (91 FR 22714). It also says the guidance is information, not law, and that it may deny any registration “inconsistent with the public interest” under 21 U.S.C. 823 (91 FR 22714; DEA). The D.C. Circuit will decide which reading holds.

In court

The November 29 cutoff is on hold for now, but only until the D.C. Circuit says otherwise.

  • The cases. State-licensed operators filed petitions for review of the October 30 guidance on November 18 and 19, 2026. The court consolidated them as Nos. 26-1324, 26-1325 and 26-1329. Petitioners in No. 26-1325 include G.P.M.II, LLC; MLH Explorations LLC; Story of PA CR, LLC; and Tri-Mountain Pure, LLC (docket, Nov. 19, 2026).
  • November 20. The court ordered the government to respond to the emergency stay motion by 4:00 p.m. on November 23 (per curiam order, No. 26-1325).
  • November 25. A panel administratively stayed the guidance as it relates to timely applicants doing state-licensed Schedule III medical marijuana business with entities that are neither registrants nor timely applicants, until further order (per curiam order, Nos. 26-1324 et al.).

What the stay does not do. It does not make a late filer “timely.” It does not decide whether the guidance is lawful. It does not touch adult-use marijuana, which stays Schedule I (DOJ press release 26-1276).

What it means for Mississippi

Mississippi dispensaries were in the first federal batch, but the growers and processors who supply them were told to wait. That gap is where the timely/untimely line has the largest effect in this state.

  • Dispensaries first. Mississippi is one of the seven states whose dispensaries received DEA registrations on October 30, 2026 (DOJ press release 26-1276).
  • Suppliers in a slower line. On August 4, 2026, the Mississippi State Department of Health told cultivation, processing, transportation and waste licensees that the DEA had no portal yet for their license types and that they could use DEA Form 225 instead (MSDH letter).
  • How the DEA classifies them. Per that letter: cultivation = bulk manufacturer; processing = manufacturer (some may be distributors); transportation = distributor; waste disposal = reverse distributor; testing labs = analytical laboratory. Vertically integrated businesses must register each licensed operation separately.
  • About 40 facilities. The Health Department reported that about 40 Mississippi cultivation, processing and transportation facilities had applied.
  • Two messages that differ. The August letter said entities holding a DEA control number could keep operating and do business with DEA-registered facilities while awaiting a decision. The October 30 DEA guidance limits that protection to those who filed by June 26 (DEA).

The practical effect: a registered Mississippi dispensary can only stock product from a grower, processor and transporter that are each registered or timely. Any link in that chain that filed after June 26 breaks it.

MSDH takes DEA registration questions in writing at MCLicensing@msdh.ms.gov, (601) 206-1540.

What comes next

The next move belongs to the D.C. Circuit, which can lift the stay, extend it, or rule on the merits. Until then, operators should keep records as if the guidance could come back.

  • Know your status. Registered, timely, or neither. Timely status turns on a complete application and fee between Apr. 28 and June 26, 2026.
  • Paper every partner. DEA submission confirmation and DCN check, or a copy of the DEA certificate; an active state license; records of each check.
  • If you haven’t applied, apply. Dispensaries use the DEA Medical Marijuana Dispensary Registration Portal; other license types, per MSDH, use DEA Form 225 and write “medical cannabis” in any freeform field.
  • Watch the broader hearing. The separate proceeding on moving all marijuana to Schedule III began June 29, 2026 (FR Doc. 2026-08177; DEA.gov).
  • Put it in writing. Questions to MSDH go to MCLicensing@msdh.ms.gov. Concerns about local effects can be sent to your congressional delegation.
